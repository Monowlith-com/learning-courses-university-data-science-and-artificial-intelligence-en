# M25-L6 – Audio, Spectrograms and Speech Recognition {#L6}

**Course area:** M25 – Computer Vision and Multimodal Learning

**Level:** Master · Compulsory elective/models

**Prerequisites:** M18, M19.

**Learning objective:** Understand time–frequency analysis and the evaluation of automatic transcription errors.

## Sampling acoustic signals {#S1}

A microphone records an announcement from a machine. The digital signal $x[n]$ consists of amplitude samples taken at sample rate $f_s$. At 16 kHz, samples are 62.5 µs apart. For a band-limited signal below the Nyquist frequency of 8 kHz, ideal reconstruction from its samples is possible. An anti-aliasing filter removes correspondingly high frequency components before sampling. Loudness, microphone characteristics, reverberation and background noise affect the data; test recordings should not simply be randomly split from the same recording session.

## Time–frequency representation {#S2}


![A synthetic signal has dominant frequency 400 Hz before 0.5 seconds and 800 Hz afterwards.](../media/img/001714_en.svg)

*Figure: Spectrogram of a frequency change.*

The short-time Fourier transform (STFT) divides the signal into overlapping windows:

$$X[m,k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[mH+n]w[n]e^{-2\pi i kn/N}.$$

$N$ is the window length, $H$ is the hop size in samples and $w$ is a window function. For example, a spectrogram displays $|X[m,k]|^2$. With $N=400$, $H=160$ and $f_s=16000$, a window lasts 25 ms, the time step is 10 ms and the frequency-bin spacing is 40 Hz. Actual frequency resolution also depends on the window. Our synthetic signal switches from 400 Hz to 800 Hz after 0.5 s. The diagram shows the change in dominant frequency region.

```python
import numpy as np
fs,N,H=16000,400,160
t=np.arange(fs)/fs
x=np.sin(2*np.pi*np.where(t<.5,400*t,800*t))
frames=np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(x,N)[::H]
P=abs(np.fft.rfft(frames*np.hanning(N),axis=1))**2
peaks=P.argmax(1)*fs/N
assert peaks[10]==400 and peaks[-10]==800
print(N/fs,H/fs,fs/N,peaks[10],peaks[-10])
```

## Learning-based speech recognition {#S3}

Mel filter banks aggregate frequency ranges on a perceptually motivated scale; their log energies are often used as inputs. An ASR (automatic speech recognition) system must convert acoustic sequences into text even though word boundaries and lengths are unknown. CTC uses a blank class and sums over valid alignments. Encoder–decoder systems can generate text autoregressively. Whisper is an example of training using large quantities of weak supervision. A language model can generate plausible words that were never spoken, so silence must be tested as well.

Word Error Rate is $\mathrm{WER}=(S+D+I)/N_{ref}$, where the terms count substitutions, deletions, insertions and reference words. Word normalisation and edit rules must be specified. The metric may exceed 100%. Speaker groups, languages, noise conditions and uncommon technical terms require separate testing.

## Exercise with solution {#S4}

The reference contains 20 words. Under the optimal edit alignment, two words are substituted, one is missing and three are inserted. WER = $(2+1+3)/20=0.30$. With the same error count but only four reference words, it would be 150%. An empty reference requires a separate convention.
