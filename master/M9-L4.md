# M9-L4 – Convexity, projections and duality {#L4}

**Course area:** M9 – Numerical methods and optimisation for learning algorithms

**Level:** Bachelor · Core/compulsory elective

**Prerequisites:** [M2-L3](M2-L3.md), [M2-L5](M2-L5.md), [M9-L1](M9-L1.md), [M9-L2](M9-L2.md).

**Learning objective:** Explain convex sets and functions, projections and primal-dual certificates.

## Why convexity is useful {#S1}

A set $C$ is convex if every line segment connecting two points in $C$ lies entirely within it. A function on a convex set is convex if, for $0\le t\le1$, the following holds:

$$f(tx+(1-t)y)\le tf(x)+(1-t)f(y).$$

A local minimum is then also global: if a point with a lower function value existed, intermediate points arbitrarily close to the local minimum would also have lower values. Strict convexity guarantees uniqueness, provided a minimum exists at all. In learning, quadratic losses and many classical regularisers are convex; deep neural networks are generally not convex in their joint parameters.

## Feasibility after every step {#S2}


![Rechteck [0,2]×[0,1], Versuch (2,7; 1,4) und Projektion auf (2; 1).](../media/img/001670_en.svg)

*Figure: Projection onto a feasible set.*

The Euclidean projection $P_C(v)=\arg\min_{z\in C}\|z-v\|_2^2$ exists and is unique for non-empty, closed, convex $C$. The projected gradient algorithm sets $w^+=P_C(w-\eta\nabla f(w))$. For the rectangle $C=[0,2]\times[0,1]$, each coordinate is clipped to its interval. Thus $(1.5,0.5)$ and an unconstrained estimate $(2.7,1.4)$ yield $(2,1)$. The diagram shows the infeasible trial point and its projection. For a general polyhedron, projection is usually itself an optimisation problem; clipping individual coordinates is then insufficient.

## Duality: obtaining a bound rather than relying on hope {#S3}

For $\min_x f(x)$ subject to $g_i(x)\le0$, the Lagrangian $\mathcal L(x,\lambda)=f(x)+\sum_i\lambda_i g_i(x)$ with $\lambda_i\ge0$ defines the dual function $q(\lambda)=\inf_x\mathcal L(x,\lambda)$. At every feasible point, $\mathcal L\le f$; therefore $q$ is a lower bound on the primal optimal value. Maximising $q$ gives the best such bound. Under suitable regularity conditions (such as Slater's condition for convex problems), strong duality can hold.

In the example $\min_x(x-3)^2$ subject to $x\le2$, $x^*=2$ and $f^*=1$. For $\lambda\ge0$, minimising $(x-3)^2+\lambda(x-2)$ gives the point $x=3-\lambda/2$ and $q(\lambda)=\lambda-\lambda^2/4$. The dual maximum occurs at $\lambda=2$ and has value one. The gap is zero. The KKT conditions are satisfied here: primal and dual feasibility, stationarity $2(x-3)+\lambda=0$ and complementary slackness $\lambda(x-2)=0$. For a convex differentiable problem, these conditions are sufficient; without suitable regularity, not every optimum need have multipliers.

## Exercise and solution {#S4}

With an upper bound of 4, $x=3$ is feasible and the constraint is inactive. With $\lambda=0$ the KKT conditions are satisfied and the optimal lower cost bound is zero. An inactive constraint needs no positive multiplier.
